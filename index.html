Station 1

Coal & Ships

How black coal and huge ships made the Northeast famous – and what happened when they disappeared.

≈ 12 minutes12:00

From black gold to empty shipyards

¶1For hundreds of years, the Northeast was famous for one thing: coal. There was so much coal under the ground that people still say “carrying coals to Newcastle” when somebody does something completely useless. Miners worked deep underground in hard and dangerous conditions, and many families lived in pit villages1 right next to the mines.

¶2Coal and iron helped another industry to grow: shipbuilding. In the 19th and 20th centuries, the River Tyne was one of the great shipbuilding rivers of the world. Thousands of men worked in shipyards2 such as Swan Hunter in Wallsend. In 1906 Swan Hunter launched3 the Mauretania – at that time the largest and fastest passenger ship in the world.

¶3After the Second World War, other countries built ships more cheaply, and Britain needed less coal. One by one, the pits and shipyards closed. In 1977 the last super-tanker left the Tyne. The last deep coal mine in the Northeast, Ellington Colliery4, closed in 2005, and Swan Hunter’s yards closed in 2007. Many people lost their jobs, and whole towns had to look for a new future5.

1pit village – a village where most people worked in the local coal mine (pit) 2shipyard – a place where ships are built 3to launch a ship – to put a new ship into the water for the first time 4colliery – a coal mine with all its buildings 5future – the time that will come

Station 2

Icons on the Tyne

Four landmarks that tell the story of a region – and one of them has a new name.

≈ 12 minutes12:00

Four icons, four stories

¶1The Tyne Bridge was opened by King George V in 1928. It is painted green, and today about 70,000 vehicles cross it every day. It was finished before the famous Sydney Harbour Bridge in Australia, which looks very similar.

¶2The Gateshead Millennium Bridge (2001) is only for people on foot and on bikes. When a ship needs to pass, the whole bridge tilts1 upwards. Because of its shape, locals call it the “Blinking Eye”.

¶3The Glasshouse is a concert hall and music school on the south bank of the river. It opened in 2004 as “Sage Gateshead” – the name in your book. In 2023 it got a new name, The Glasshouse International Centre for Music, because a new arena next door took the name “Sage”. Its walls are made of 630 panes2 of glass.

¶4The Angel of the North by the artist Antony Gormley was put up in 1998. It is 20 metres high, its wings are 54 metres wide, and it weighs about 200 tonnes. It stands on the site of an old colliery. Gormley said that the Angel remembers the miners who worked under the ground there for two hundred years. It also shows the change from the industrial3 age to the information age.

1to tilt – to move so that one side is higher than the other 2pane – one piece of glass in a window 3industrial – connected with factories, mines and heavy work

Station 3

Why aye, man!

People from Tyneside are called Geordies – and so is the way they speak. You might understand more than you think.

≈ 12 minutes12:00

A very old way of speaking

¶1An accent is the way you pronounce words. A dialect is more: it has its own words and sometimes its own grammar. Geordie is both – a strong accent and a dialect.

¶2Many Geordie words are very old. In the 5th century, the Angles came to Britain from what is today northern Germany and Denmark, and some of their words are still used on Tyneside. A Geordie calls a child a bairn – in Danish it is barn. To go is gan – compare it with German gehen. And home is hyem.

¶3The most famous Geordie word is probably howay. It means “come on!” – and you can hear it every Saturday when thousands of fans cheer for Newcastle United, the football club of “the Toon”1.

1the Toon – Geordie for “the town”: Newcastle and its city centre
Listen out! Geordies often call a friend marra and a woman or girl hinny (“honey”). If something is canny, it is good or nice.

Station 4

Walls, Vikings & Stars

Leave the city behind: Romans, monks, Vikings, castles and some of the darkest skies in Europe.

≈ 12 minutes12:00

2,000 years of history – and a lot of sky

¶1Hadrian’s Wall. In AD 122, the Roman emperor Hadrian ordered his army to build a stone wall right across northern Britain. It is about 117 km (73 miles) long and runs from coast to coast. Its eastern end is in a town on the Tyne which is still called Wallsend. There, you can visit the old Roman fort1 Segedunum. Since 1987 the wall has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

¶2Lindisfarne. North of Newcastle lies Lindisfarne, also called Holy Island. You can only drive there at low tide2 on a road through the sea – at high tide, the road is under water, and every year some visitors get stuck. In 793, Vikings attacked the monastery3 on the island. The news shocked people all over Europe. Not far away is Bamburgh, once home of the kings of Northumbria.

¶3Dark skies. Northumberland National Park and Kielder Water & Forest Park form one of the largest Dark Sky Parks in Europe. There is so little artificial light that on a clear night you can see the Milky Way. Kielder Water is the largest man-made lake in northern Europe.

1fort – a strong building for soldiers 2tide – the rise and fall of the sea (low tide / high tide) 3monastery – a place where monks live and work

Station 5

The Northeast today

After coal and ships: cars, games, culture – and a German twin town.

≈ 12 minutes12:00

New jobs, new ideas

¶1When the mines and shipyards closed, the Northeast had to reinvent itself. In 1986, the Japanese company Nissan opened a car factory in Sunderland, on the site of an old airfield1. Today around 6,000 people work there, and the plant also builds electric cars.

¶2The region has also become a centre for video games. More than 60 games companies are based here – for example Ubisoft Reflections in Newcastle. Five universities, hospitals, shops, tourism and culture, like the Glasshouse or the BALTIC art gallery, give many people work.

¶3Newcastle has a German twin town2: Gelsenkirchen in the Ruhr area. The two cities became partners in 1948 – because both lived from coal. Today both have had to find new jobs for the people who once worked in the mines.

1airfield – a small airport 2twin town – a town that has a special friendship with a town in another country
Lübeck connection: Lübeck had a big shipyard too. The Flender shipyard built nearly 700 ships – and closed in 2002, just a few years before Swan Hunter on the Tyne.
